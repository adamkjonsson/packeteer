# Plan — v0.16.0: the receiver's release

*Written 2026-09-16.*

*Status: all four issues done — #158 as PR #159, #160 and #161 as PR #162,
#163 on `v_0.16.0`. The sweep and the release remain.*

0.15.0 pointed packeteer at traffic it did not write and found three bugs in
the round trip. This release is about the other seat: every issue in it is
about what a **receiver** sees, and three of the four exist because the
corpus's one lossy capture was taken at the sender. From a reassembler's
seat nothing in `tcp_lossy_ts.pcap` was ever missing — each resend is a pure
overlap — so a downstream project measuring what 0.15.0 let it test
(python-zipline-wire#17) found it could not exercise a gap at all.

**The captures.** #158 asks for the receiver-side twin of that file, and
floats a second one for reordering. #160 asks for the same session with
*corruption* as the loss, so that two copies of one range genuinely differ.
#161 asks for a session whose sequence numbers wrap through 2³². All three
came from one script, in one namespace topology, and all three are collected,
verified, tested and committed.

**The generator.** #163 is what the corpus found this time: the generated
receiver acknowledges bytes it rejected. `_apply_corruption` corrupts a
segment and moves exactly one ACK behind the retransmission; every later ACK
is cumulative and covers the corrupted range, so a consumer that reads ACK
numbers is told the receiver kept the corrupt bytes. `tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap` is
what a receiver actually does in that situation, and it is the reference the
fix is measured against — the same relationship #150 had to #149's file.

```
#158 → #160 + #161 → #163 → docs sweep → release
```

- **#158 first.** It built the instrument: the three-namespace script with a
  receiver-side capture point and a check that replays each file through a
  receiver's eyes before reporting success. #160 and #161 are each one more
  mode of it.
- **#160 and #161 together.** Same script, same commit; #161 is the lower
  priority of the two by its own account, but it cost nothing extra to ship
  beside #160 once the ISN-clock method worked.
- **#163 after #160, and last.** It is the only issue that changes generated
  bytes, so every seeded fixture is touched in one window — 0.14.0 and 0.15.0
  made the same argument for #131, #90 and #150. It also wants the real
  capture landed first: "what does a receiver acknowledge while a corrupted
  segment's hole is open" is a question `tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap` answers in

  from the RFC. **If the milestone has to ship before it is finished, #163 is

Sizing, honestly: the three captures took two days including two wrong turns

receiver model of perhaps sixty lines, and the rest is fixtures, the tests
that should have caught it, and the documentation that described the wrong

# Part 1 — #158, the gap from the receiver's seat

## What the captures are

Two files, both from `testcases/real/collect/capture_tcp_gap.sh`: the same
client–router–server namespaces as `tcp_lossy_ts.pcap`, `netem delay 20ms` on
both legs, and tcpdump on the **client's** device, downstream of the
impairment.

| File | Packets | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `tcp_gap_ts.pcap` | 215 | 10% loss on the router's client-facing leg |
| `tcp_reorder_ts.pcap` | 267 | ~1 in 16 segments down a 70 ms band instead of 20 ms |

## What is true of them

| #158's assertion | `tcp_gap_ts` | `tcp_reorder_ts` |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A hole a later segment fills | 9 holes, 12 fills; the dropped segment is absent | 4 holes, 8 fills; the original is what fills |
| 2. Fill's TSval against what was committed past it | **newer** than everything, 12/12 (9 strictly, 3 same-tick ties) | **older** than everything, 8/8 (2 strictly) |
| 3. The receiver's ACK answering the fill echoes it | 12/12 | 8/8 |

Between them they are the two answers a late byte can have — real loss
recovered after the gap was committed, or a reordering the capture point saw —
which is #17's third item.

### What #158 corrected

**The second file is not `netem reorder`, and cannot be.** A veth sender puts
a whole congestion window on the wire inside one 1 ms TSval tick, and netem's
reorder only lets a packet jump ahead of what is queued in front of it — its
own burst-mates, which share its TSval. The first attempt produced 57 fills,
every one a same-tick tie. An original that arrives with an *older* TSval than
its successors has to be held back past the *next* burst, which is what the
two-band `prio` qdisc with a `u32` match on the sequence number does; and
because the sender's fast retransmit carries the same sequence number it takes
the slow band too, arriving after the original as a repeat the receiver
D-SACKs. That is now detail 5 in `MANIFEST.md`'s method section.

**tcpdump lost the tail, and the file looked complete.** TPACKET_V3 holds
packets in a block for up to a second; stopping tcpdump half a second after
the transfer wrote 165 of 198 packets, and only tcpdump's own summary said so.
`--immediate-mode`, a two-second settle, and a check that the two numbers in
the log agree — detail 4.

**The classification changed twice before it was right.** Comparing a fill
against every held segment called three good `tcp_gap_ts` fills ties, because
the sender puts new data out in the same tick as a resend; comparing against
the next segment only called six good `tcp_reorder_ts` fills ties, because
the immediately following segment is a burst-mate. The right lens is
"nothing committed past it is newer" against "nothing committed past it is
older", with the ties reported and not counted.

# Part 2 — #160 and #161, two more modes

## What the captures are

| File | Packets | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap` | 196 | `netem corrupt 3%`; TX checksum offload and scatter-gather off at the sender |
| `tcp_wrap_ts.pcap` | 198 | ISN steered to 36 226 bytes below 2³²; the last 2 KiB before the wrap on the slow band |

## What is true of them

**#160.** 5 segments reach the receiver with **one payload bit flipped** and a
TCP checksum that fails; the other 191 checksums verify. Each is dropped,
resent clean with a later TSval, and the ACK covering the in-order resend
echoes it, 5/5. The only file in the corpus where two copies of one range
differ in their bytes, and the only one where a checksum failure is a fact
about the wire. `sanitise` keeps it that way: `parse` records a checksum only
when it was wrong on the wire, so the sanitised copy has exactly 5 bad and
191 recomputed good.

**#161.** 74 segments below 2³² and 56 above; the receiver's ACK numbers wrap
with them and its TSecr never decreases. The four originals from just below
the wrap arrive after all 56 post-wrap segments with older TSvals — the one
case where sequence number and TSval disagree and the TSval is right. Their
fast retransmits arrive after the receiver has closed and draw four RSTs, the
only RSTs in the corpus's TCP sessions.

### What #160 and #161 corrected

**`netem corrupt` only damages headers unless scatter-gather is off.** It
flips a bit in the skb's *linear* part, and Linux TCP keeps payload in page
frags: the first attempt put four of four flips in headers (an IP destination,
a TSval, a TCP header, an ethertype). `ethtool -K vs sg off` makes the stack
linearise every skb at transmit — detail 6.

**A wrap is a matter of timing, not luck, once the right queue is read.**
Linux's ISN is `siphash(4-tuple) + (realtime_ns >> 6)`, so for a fixed
4-tuple it climbs at 15.625 M/s and sweeps 2³² every ~275 s; probe once,
wait, connect on time. Two attempts landed a constant 2.8 s short with the
rate exactly nominal, which was not the clock: `TCP_RECV_QUEUE` is 1, not 2,
and the probe had been reading the client's own `write_seq` — the two ISNs
share the clock and differ by a constant hash. A third landed 3.5 ms late
because a VM oversleeps, so the last 50 ms are spun — detail 7.

**The corpus size cap went from 512 KiB to 1 MiB.** Each receiver-side
capture is a whole 64 KiB transfer, which is what it takes for a stack to show
a fast retransmit, and a slice would lose the handshake that tells a reader
the ISN. The test's docstring records the reason.

**The test replay uses serial arithmetic.** It did not need to until
`tcp_wrap_ts.pcap`, and the change is what makes #161's third assertion
statable: numerically the delayed original is *larger* than everything held
beyond it, serially it is before them.

# Part 3 — #163, a receiver that acknowledges what it rejected

## The bug, precisely

`_apply_corruption` runs after the connection is emitted. It flips a byte in
segment *i*, appends a clean retransmission an RTO later, and moves the one
ACK that `_ack_positions` pairs with *i* — the ACK whose number is
`seq + payload_len` — to just behind the retransmission. Nothing else moves.

Reproduced with the issue's parameters (seed 2, 40 data packets,
`payload_corruption_probability=0.2`, `retransmission_probability=0.3`),
reading the packets back in timestamp order:

```
CORRUPT[0]  pkt 3:  RETRANS at pkt 73;  28/28 intervening ACKs cover it
CORRUPT[2]  pkt 6:  RETRANS at pkt 75;  27/28 intervening ACKs cover it
CORRUPT[3]  pkt 7:  RETRANS at pkt 78;  27/29 intervening ACKs cover it
…
14 corruptions, 14 acknowledged past before their resend
```

The first covering ACK after `CORRUPT[0]` is `ACK[1]`, with an ack number
1 754 bytes past the corrupted segment's start, echoing `DATA[1]`'s TSval —
neither copy's. A receiver that dropped `CORRUPT[0]` on its checksum would
have answered `DATA[1]` with a duplicate ACK at `CORRUPT[0]`'s start, echoing
the TSval of the last segment that arrived *in order*.

**There is a second defect the issue did not name.** The moved ACK keeps its
original number — `seq + payload_len` of the corrupted segment alone — so
after `RETRANS[3]` the stream carries an ACK covering 113 bytes past the
resend's start, when a receiver that had held twenty-seven later segments
would jump to cover all of them. Since every ACK before it already covered
more, the moved ACK's number is *lower* than its predecessors: an
acknowledgement that goes backwards, which is not traffic that could have
happened. `apply_loss_recovery` gets this right with `_contiguous_ack`; the
corruption pass never calls it.

## Why nothing caught it

The tests for corruption assert that a `CORRUPT[n]` exists, that its payload
differs from `RETRANS[n]`'s, and that the acknowledgement paired with it now
follows the retransmission. Nothing reads the ack number of any *other*
packet, and nothing asserts the invariant that a direction's ack numbers never
decrease. The loss path is right because its receiver model runs at emit
time, inside `emit()`, where `dst.ack` advances only for an in-order arrival
and `dst.ts_recent` follows it; the corruption pass is a post-pass on a
finished timeline and has no model at all, only one ACK to move.

## The fix

A receiver model that runs over a finished timeline: given the packets in
time order and a predicate saying which data segments *arrived*, walk them and
maintain `rcv_nxt` and `TS.Recent` per direction exactly as `emit()` does,
then rewrite the ack number and TSecr of every acknowledgement the reply
direction sends. A corrupted original did not arrive; its retransmission did.
The rewritten ACKs then say what a receiver says: duplicates at the hole's
start while it is open, echoing the last in-order TSval; a jump to cover
everything contiguous once the resend lands, echoing the resend's TSval —
the shape `tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap` holds, 5 of 5.

`_contiguous_ack` is most of the arithmetic already, and wants only an
*arrived* filter instead of "every segment in the list".

### <a name="q1"></a>Q1 — a post-pass receiver model, or corruption at emit time

The issue suggests reusing the loss machinery, which lives in `emit()`.
Moving corruption there would make it a per-segment decision like
`drop_packet`, with the corrupted copy emitted and the receiver told it did
not arrive — `lost_segments` would then carry it and `apply_loss_recovery`
would do the rest.

But `apply_impairments` is shared: `payloads/http.py` applies the same passes
to a session rendered by `render_tcp_session`, where the server's response
segments carry ACK numbers of their own. A fix inside the low-level `emit()`
would fix one generator of two.

*Recommendation: the post-pass model.* It fixes both callers, it is
assertable against the real file in exactly the form the corpus tests already
use (`_replay` in `test_real_corpus.py` *is* this model, read-only), and it
gives the corruption pass the thing it was missing rather than moving the
pass to where a model happens to exist. `apply_loss_recovery` could later be
re-expressed on the same model; that is not this release.

### <a name="q2"></a>Q2 — rewrite bare ACKs only, or the ack field of every reply-direction segment

The low-level generator only has bare ACKs in the reply direction, so the two
readings agree there. On the HTTP path a server response segment carries the
receiver's cumulative acknowledgement in its own header, and a client request
corrupted in flight would still be "acknowledged" by the response that
follows it if only bare ACKs were rewritten.

*Recommendation: every segment.* The ack field is receiver state regardless
of what carries it. Rewriting a data segment's ack means rebuilding its frame,
which `_restamped` already knows how to do for a timestamp change.

### <a name="q3"></a>Q3 — what the ACK that fills the hole is called

The loss path labels its jump `ACK-RECOVER[n]`. The corruption pass leaves the
moved ACK as `ACK[n]`, which after this fix is the segment whose number
jumps.

*Recommendation: `ACK-RECOVER[n]`, matching the loss path*, and the duplicate
ACKs keep their `ACK[k]` labels — each is still the acknowledgement the
receiver sent for `DATA[k]`, with the number a receiver would have put in it.
This is a label change on a seeded stream and goes in the changelog. The
issue's own assertion is written in terms of these labels and should stay
true of them: for every `CORRUPT[n]`, no `ACK[*]` between it and
`RETRANS[n]` has `ack > seq(CORRUPT[n])`.

### A note on `_tsecr_at`

`_stream_common._tsecr_at` documents itself as `TS.Recent` and returns the
most recent TSval that *arrived* by a given time, in-order or not. Where it is
used today — a retransmission's own TSecr, and the ACK-RECOVER after one — the
latest arrival happens to be in order, so it is right. It is not a receiver
model, and the ACKs the new pass rewrites take their TSecr from the model, not
from it. Its docstring should say what it actually returns.

## Tests

- **The issue's property**, on the low-level generator and on the HTTP one:
  for every `CORRUPT[n]`, every reply-direction segment between it and
  `RETRANS[n]` has `ack ≤ seq(CORRUPT[n])`, and the first after `RETRANS[n]`
  covers it.
- **The echo**: duplicate ACKs while the hole is open echo the TSval of the
  last in-order arrival; the recovering ACK echoes the retransmission's.
- **The invariant that would have caught the second defect**: within a
  direction, ack numbers never decrease (mod 2³²), asserted over every
  impairment combination the existing tests already generate. This is the
  test whose absence is the actual bug.
- **Against the reference**: write a generated corrupted stream to a pcap,
  read it back through `packeteer.parse`, and run the same receiver replay
  `TestACorruptedSegmentAndItsResend` runs on `tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap` — the
  damaged copy opens a hole, the resend fills it with a newer TSval, the
  covering ACK echoes the resend. A generator whose receiver passes the real
  file's tests is the claim this release makes.
- **Fixtures**: every seeded test that asserts on a corrupted stream's bytes
  regenerates. Each is inspected before it is accepted, as #150's were: a
  fixture that changed for a reason other than acknowledgement numbers is a
  finding.

## Changelog

Under `Fixed`, and it says plainly what a consumer saw: the generated receiver
acknowledged bytes it had rejected, and any policy reading ack numbers against
a corrupted segment was told the wrong thing. The label change (Q3) is named.
Generated bytes change for any seeded stream with `payload_corruption_probability`
set, and that is said too.

## Documentation

Three pages describe the old behaviour as the intended one and need the new
one instead:

- `docs/internals/stream-generators.md`, the impairment table row: *"The ACK
  for that packet is delayed to follow a retransmission"* — now: the receiver
  treats it as lost, duplicates until the resend, jumps after it.
- `docs/cli/stream.md` around line 243, and `docs/guide/generating.md` around
  line 219, which explain what a corrupted segment looks like next to its
  resend and should now say what the receiver does in between.

### What #163 corrected

**Q1 settled as recommended, and the model is smaller than feared.**
`_reacknowledge` is a walk over the packets in time order keeping `rcv_nxt`
and `TS.Recent` per direction — the same two rules `emit()` applies — with a
set of indices that never arrived. It rebuilds only the packets whose
acknowledgement or echo actually differ, so a timeline the emit-time receiver
already got right comes back byte-identical, and it runs only when a
corruption happened. The suite's only fixtures it reached were the two
assertions naming the moved ACK's label.

**Q2 narrowed, deliberately.** The first cut rewrote every ACK-bearing
segment, and the one-segment no-timestamps test failed with a retransmission
three bytes off its original: the resend went out after the server's FIN had
arrived, so the model gave it the client's *current* acknowledgement. A real
stack does that too — but that is the sender's retransmission, which #90
promised is the original byte for byte, and #163 is about the receiver's
acknowledgements. The rewrite is scoped to the acknowledgements *of a
direction that lost something*: every packet travelling the other way, data or
not, which on the HTTP path still reaches a piggybacked response. The
sender-side stale ack on a retransmission is real, pre-dates this issue on the
loss path too, and is listed below rather than folded in.

**Q3 as recommended**: `ACK-RECOVER[n]`, and the two tests that looked for
`ACK[n]` behind the retransmission were the fixture change.

**The tests were seen to fail.** Against the pre-fix pass, the issue's
property fails on both generators (21 subtests), the never-decreasing
invariant fails 24 times — that is the backwards ACK — and the echo, jump and
wrap tests all fail. The strongest of them is the last class: it subclasses
`tcp_corrupt_ts.pcap`'s own test case, writes a generated corrupted stream to
a pcap, reads it back through the parser, and runs the real file's assertions
on it, with one override — packeteer flips a byte where netem flips a bit.

**`_contiguous_ack` was wrong at the wrap, and it was cheap to show.** With the
integer comparison restored, a lossy stream whose ISN sits 5 000 bytes below
2³² leaves its last `ACK-RECOVER` at 4 294 966 688, just under the wrap; with
serial arithmetic it reaches the end of the data. Fixed here, under its own
changelog entry, since the model needed the helper anyway.

**The invariant test has a carve-out that names a real approximation.** Ack
numbers never decrease *except* on retransmitted copies and duplicates of
them, whose acknowledgement field is the original's and can be stale by the
time they go out. The docstring says so, and so does the list below.

## Not in this milestone

- **#127** stays open. Its gap list is unchanged by this release — MPLS,
  GTP-U, an FCS, IPsec, a compressed DNS message, an ICMPv4 Redirect — since
  every capture here is plain Ethernet TCP.
- **#115**, conditional on reassembly being revisited. Nothing here does.
- **A retransmission carries its original's acknowledgement field.** By
  the time a segment is resent, the peer may have sent more — its FIN,
  typically — and the copy's ack number is then stale, lower than the ack
  numbers around it. `_restamped` keeps the original's bytes by #90's rule,
  and the loss path has always done the same. A real stack puts the current
  `rcv_nxt` in every segment it sends, retransmissions included; making the
  generator do that changes bytes for every seeded retransmission fixture and
  the "verbatim without timestamps" promise, so it is an issue of its own.
- **Re-expressing `apply_loss_recovery` on the #163 receiver model.** The two
  would then be one mechanism, which is the right end state; but the loss path
  is correct today and the change would touch every seeded loss fixture for no
  behavioural gain. It goes in the issue tracker, not this milestone.
- **A generated wrap under impairments.** `TestSeqWrapAround` covers one
  clean segment across 2³². It does not cover loss recovery there, and
  `_contiguous_ack` compares `begin <= edge < end` as plain integers, so a
  segment straddling the wrap has `end < begin` and never extends the edge —
  a recovering ACK after a wrap would stop short. The #163 receiver model
  should be written in serial arithmetic from the start, which is the moment
  to give `_contiguous_ack` the same; but asserting the loss path across a
  wrap is its own issue, filed rather than folded in.
